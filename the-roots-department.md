# The Roots Department

---

*For my mother —*

*who I love a lot, more than fits in a sentence.*

*This story is for you.*

## Chapter 1 — The Queue

Maya's hands knew the keyboard better than they knew anything else. Eight hours a day, five days a week, her fingers moved across the keys without looking, the way other people's hands know a piano, or bread dough, or soil.

She worked on the fourth floor of a glass building, in the call center of a company called OriginTree. The company sold the past. You paid, you spat into a small plastic tube, you mailed the tube away, and six weeks later the past came back to you as a map with colored circles on it. *You are 31% this. You are 22% that.* People paid a lot of money for those circles.

Maya's job was to answer the phone when the circles were not enough.

"Thank you for calling OriginTree, my name is Maya, how can I help you today?"

She said this sentence approximately sixty times a day. She had said it so many times that the words had stopped being words. They were just sounds now, a small song her mouth performed while her mind stood somewhere behind her, watching.

On her screen there was a number that never slept. It showed how many customers were waiting in the queue. On a quiet morning the number was four or five. On a Monday after a holiday sale, it could climb past two hundred, and then a red light blinked above the floor like a small angry heart, and the team leaders walked between the desks saying, *Keep it moving, keep it moving.*

The customers in the queue were all waiting to ask, in one way or another, the same question: *Who am I?*

This was the strange thing about her job, and Maya tried not to think about it too often. Strangers called her — her, Maya, twenty-six years old, a headset pressing a line into her hair — and asked her to explain who they were. She would open their file, look at their circles, and read the past to them like a weather report.

"Yes, sir, I see it here. Your results show approximately forty percent from the Iberian Peninsula."

"Forty percent? My mother always said we were Irish."

"There is some of that too, sir. Eleven percent."

"Eleven? Only eleven?"

People got aggravated about percentages. This always surprised the new workers, but it did not surprise Maya anymore. A man could live happily for fifty years, and then a colored circle on a screen would tell him he was eleven percent of something instead of forty, and his whole voice would change. Some customers cried. Some shouted. One woman demanded a refund because the test had found Norwegian ancestors and she had already bought a very expensive Scottish skirt.

Maya was good at pacifying them. It was, her manager said, her special talent. She knew how to slow her voice down, how to leave small soft spaces between her sentences, how to say *I understand* in a way that sounded like a hand on a shoulder. The angry customers grew calm. The crying customers blew their noses and thanked her. At the end of each call, an automatic questionnaire was sent to the customer: *How would you rate your service today, from one to five?*

Maya's rate was 4.9.

There was a board on the wall of the call center, a big screen that showed the ranks of all forty agents, updated every hour. Maya had been first for seven months. Her name sat at the top of the list in green letters: **M. ALON — 4.9**. Below her, the other names moved up and down like swimmers in a pool, but her name did not move. The team leaders pointed at it during training sessions. *This is what we want,* they said. *Be like Maya.*

Maya looked at her name on the board sometimes and felt nothing at all.

The company liked to keep track of everything. Calls per hour. Seconds per call. Smiles per call — yes, smiles; there was a theory that customers could hear a smile through the phone, so the agents were asked to smile while they talked, and a poster near the coffee machine showed a cartoon sun wearing a headset. Maya smiled into the phone all day at people who could not see her face. By five o'clock her cheeks were tired in a place she could not name.

In the breaks, the other agents stood by the windows and talked about their weekends, about apartments they could not afford, about a new café that had opened downstairs. Nadav from the second row was trying to get everyone to sign up for a running group. "It will change your life," he said, holding his paper cup. "Sunday mornings. The sea. Come on, Maya, I'll sign you up."

"Maybe," said Maya, which was her way of saying no slowly.

The truth was that her Sunday mornings were already taken.

Every Sunday, Maya took two buses south, out of the city, to a small street where the houses were old and low and the gardens still touched each other over the fences. At the end of the street, behind a green gate that had to be lifted a little before it would open, lived her grandmother Rosa.

Rosa did not have a file at OriginTree. She had never spat into a tube. If you asked her where the family came from, she would wave her hand at the air as if she were brushing away a fly, and say, "From far. Eat."

Maya knew the names of her customers' great-great-grandmothers. She knew which village in Sicily, which port, which ship. She could trace a stranger's family back two hundred years in four clicks.

About her own family, she knew almost nothing.

She knew her grandmother's hands. She knew the garden. She knew the smell of the soup. On the fourth floor of the glass building, between one call and the next, with the queue blinking red and the cartoon sun smiling above the coffee machine, this had always seemed like enough.

It was the spring when everything changed, although nothing looked like change at first. At first it looked like an ordinary Sunday, and a pot of soup, and one small word that came out wrong.

## Chapter 2 — Sunday at Rosa's

The green gate had a trick to it. You had to lift it a little, push with your hip, and then it opened with a long complaint, like an old man getting out of a chair. Maya had known the trick since she was six. Her hands did it now without thinking, the same way they typed.

Behind the gate, the garden began at once. There was no path of stones, no clean line between one thing and another. The garden simply happened to you. Tomato plants leaned against the fence on wooden sticks, heavy with fruit in every stage of ripeness — some green and hard, some orange, some so ripe they had split their own skins in the sun and stood there shining, open, almost embarrassing. Mint grew where it wanted. A lemon tree held up one corner of the sky. And in the deep shade under the fig tree, if you were quiet and lucky, you could see the toad.

The toad had lived in the garden longer than anyone could prove. Rosa claimed it was the same toad from thirty years ago. Maya's mother said toads do not live thirty years, and Rosa said, "Mine does," and that was the end of the discussion.

"Savta?" Maya called.

"In the kitchen. Wipe your feet, the garden is on them."

The garden was always on everything. That was the point of it, Maya thought. The whole small house smelled of soil and lemons and, underneath, of something older — the cool, mineral smell of clay.

Rosa had been a potter. Not as a hobby — as a life. For forty years there had been a wheel in the back room and a small kiln in the yard, and Rosa's pots had gone out into the world to hold other people's flowers and salt and ashes. The wheel was covered with a sheet now. Her hands, she said, had retired before the rest of her. But the pots remained, everywhere, on every shelf and step: bowls, jugs, plates, cups with thumb-dents in their handles exactly where a thumb wanted to be. Growing up, Maya had thought all dishes were like this — heavy, warm-colored, each one slightly different. The first time she ate at a friend's house, the white factory plates had frightened her a little. They were so light. They seemed to be pretending not to exist.

In the kitchen, Rosa stood at the stove, stirring.

She was small now, smaller every year, as if she were slowly returning to a more concentrated form of herself. Her face was a map of wrinkles, and like a map it told you things if you knew how to read it: here laughter, here sun, here two wars and a sea. Her forearms were still a potter's forearms. When she stirred the pot, she stirred from the shoulder.

"Taste," she said, instead of hello.

This was the law of the house. Maya took the wooden spoon and tasted. Soup — orange, thick, with lentils and carrots and the soft secret of cumin underneath.

"It needs nothing," Maya said.

"It needs five more minutes. Sit."

Maya sat. This, too, was the law. In Rosa's kitchen you sat, you were given things, and you answered questions while Rosa stood with her back to you, adding seasoning to a pot that needed nothing, listening with her whole spine.

"So. The work."

"The work is the work."

"They still make you smile at the telephone?"

"They still do."

Rosa made a sound in her throat that needed no translation. To her, Maya's job was one of the great comedies of the modern world. *People pay money to be told where their grandmother was born. They should call their grandmother. Cheaper.*

"And you eat? At this work?"

"There's a cafeteria."

"A cafeteria," Rosa repeated, the way other people might say *a swamp*. She turned with the spoon in her hand. "When your mother was your age, she also thrived on air and coffee. Then she fainted in a bank. Did she ever tell you she fainted in a bank?"

"Forty times."

"Good. Then faint in a bank, who am I to talk." But she was smiling, the wrinkles rearranging themselves into their happiest pattern, and she put bread on the table, and a clay bowl of oil, and tomatoes that had been part of the garden ten minutes ago.

They ate. Sun moved across the table in slow stripes. Rosa told the news of the street: the neighbors' son who had finally found work, the cat that was apparently pregnant again, the woman from number twelve who had cut down a healthy orange tree, an act Rosa described in the voice of a war correspondent. Maya told small safe stories from the call center — the woman with the Scottish skirt, the man who wanted to be more Italian. Rosa laughed until she had to put her spoon down.

"And your tomatoes," Maya said. "The crop is good this year?"

"The crop is a scandal. Come."

After lunch they went out, as always, into the garden, and Rosa walked the rows touching things. This leaf, this stick, this fruit. She did not exactly inspect them. It was more like a doctor's rounds, or a queen greeting her citizens. The soil here was dark and fertile, fed for fifty years on kitchen water and coffee grounds and eggshells, and everything that touched it seemed to thrive almost against its will. The fig tree alone gave more fruit than three families could eat. Figs lay in the shade beneath it, ripe to the edge of decay, and wasps wrote slow circles above them.

"Take figs when you go," said Rosa. "Take tomatoes. Take mint."

"Savta, I have one bag."

"So you'll come back Thursday for the rest."

By the fence, Maya crouched to look under the fig tree, into the cool green dark. Two golden eyes looked back, patient and ancient.

"He's still here," she said.

"Where would he go?" said Rosa. "He has everything. Shade, water, flies, a view." She leaned on the fence. "He's no fool, the — "

And there she stopped.

It was a very small thing. A hole in the sentence, no bigger than a word. Rosa stood with her hand on the fence, looking at the toad, and Maya watched her search — watched her actually search, the way you search a drawer, lifting things, putting them aside — for the word *toad*.

"The prince," Rosa said finally, and waved her hand at the air. "He's no fool, the prince."

She said it like a joke. Maybe it even was a joke. The afternoon continued; figs were picked; the bag was filled past arguing. At the green gate Rosa held Maya's face in both hands, as she always did, and said, "Eat something that isn't coffee," as she always did.

On the first bus home, Maya decided it had been a joke. *The prince.* It was funny. It was exactly Rosa's kind of funny.

On the second bus, with the bag of figs warm on her knees, she stopped deciding and just looked out the window.

Sixty times a day, strangers asked Maya to find their lost words for them — names, villages, ships. She knew better than anyone how a word could fall out of the world.

She kept the figs on her kitchen table for a week, until they passed from ripeness into ruin, and she could not have said why she did not eat them.

## Chapter 3 — The Free Kit

In April, the company launched a new campaign. It was called **Kickstart Your Story**, and for three weeks it was everywhere: on the walls of the elevator, in everyone's inbox, on little cards that appeared beside the coffee machine under the smiling cartoon sun. The posters showed a young woman on a mountain at sunrise, holding the plastic tube up to the light as if it were a torch.

*Your story didn't start with you,* said the posters. *Find out where. Kickstart your story today.*

"Her story started with spit," said Tomer, who sat to Maya's left and was counting the days to the end of his contract. "Beautiful sunrise, though."

As part of the campaign, every employee could order one kit for free. Human Resources sent the link three times. *Don't miss out! Offer ends Friday!* By Wednesday, most of the floor had signed up — partly from curiosity, partly because refusing free things requires energy, and nobody at the call center had energy to spare.

Maya did not sign up on Wednesday. She could not have explained why. She read the email, moved her mouse toward the link, and then a call came in, and then another, and the day swallowed the question the way it swallowed everything.

On Thursday, Mr. Mandelbaum called again.

Mr. Mandelbaum was famous on the fourth floor. He was eighty-one years old, he lived alone in Haifa, and he had bought a kit fourteen months ago. His results had arrived long ago and had been explained to him, in full, many times, by many agents. This did not stop him from calling. He called every week, sometimes twice, always with a small official question that took one minute, followed by forty minutes of everything else.

The new agents found him difficult, because he ruined their call-time statistics. The team leaders had a special code for him in the system. The veterans had a joke: the queue at OriginTree was Mr. Mandelbaum's hangout. Other men his age had a bench in the park, a corner at the café. He had a 1-800 number and infinite patience.

That Thursday, the system sent him to Maya.

"OriginTree, my name is Maya, how — "

"Maya!" he said, delighted, as if they had met on a street corner after years. "The famous Maya. They gave me the champion."

"How can I help you today, Mr. Mandelbaum?"

"A small question. In my results, it says 2% Caucasus. My question is the following: is this a lot?"

It was not a lot. He knew it was not a lot. He had asked whether it was a lot in February. Maya pulled up his file anyway, and looked again at his circles, and told him what 2% might mean, and he listened with deep satisfaction, the way people listen to a weather report for a city where someone they love lives.

Then, the question finished, he settled in.

He told her that his neighbor's grandson had fixed his computer and now the letters were enormous, biblical. He told her that the sea in Haifa had been the color of a knife all week. He told her — apparently this was the real reason for the call, arrived at after twenty minutes, the way a ship arrives at a port — that his late wife's birthday had been on Sunday, and that he had ordered a kit for his daughter in Toronto, and that his daughter had not yet spat in the tube. "Eight months, the tube is sitting in her kitchen," he said. "What does it cost her? A little spit. But the young are busy."

The queue counter on Maya's screen blinked: eleven waiting. Twelve. Her statistics were dying quietly in front of her. The smart thing, the trained thing, was to wrap up: *Is there anything else I can help you with today?* — the sentence that ends conversations the way a drain ends bathwater.

"Maybe she's afraid," Maya heard herself say instead.

A pause on the line.

"Afraid," said Mr. Mandelbaum. "Of what? It's spit, not surgery."

"Afraid of what the answer changes. Or afraid the answer matters less to her than it does to you, and that you'll be able to tell." Maya was looking at the queue and not seeing it. "People are not busy for eight months. People are slow when something is heavy."

The silence lasted long enough that she wondered if the call had dropped.

"You are not like the others, the champions," Mr. Mandelbaum said at last. "The others are polite. You're listening." He said it the way one says a diagnosis. Then, cheerfully: "So. The Caucasus. Two percent. It's the mountains, you say?"

When the call ended — twenty-eight minutes, a small massacre of her hourly average — Maya sat for a moment with her hands in her lap. Around her the floor hummed: forty voices reading the past aloud to strangers. On her other monitor, the HR email still waited. *Offer ends Friday!*

She thought about Rosa's hand on the fence, searching the air for a word the size of a toad.

She thought: it is not for me. I don't care about circles. It's a distraction, a toy, a sunrise made of spit.

She thought: Rosa will never do it. Rosa waves her hand at the air. *From far. Eat.* And one day — this thought arrived whole and cold, like something that had been waiting a long time in a back room — one day the wave of the hand will be all that's left. Whatever Rosa knows and does not say, it is written in exactly one place, and the place is Rosa.

Maya clicked the link. She filled in her name, her address, her employee number. It took ninety seconds. There was a final screen with confetti drawn on it: *Congratulations! You just kickstarted your story!*

"Welcome to the cult," said Tomer, without looking over.

The kit arrived at her apartment four days later in a white box the size of a book. Inside: the tube, a funnel, instructions in eleven languages, and a small folded questionnaire about her family's history. *Where were your four grandparents born? (If known.)*

She sat at her kitchen table with a pen and got no further than her own name. The figs were long gone from the table; their absence had left a faint sweet ghost. She skipped the questionnaire. She spat into the tube — an undignified process the posters had wisely not shown — sealed it, and put it by the door so she would not forget it in the morning.

It stood there in the dark, a small white tower with the whole sleeping past inside it, and Maya lay in bed and could not sleep, and could not say why, and at some point past midnight she understood that she was not doing this instead of asking Rosa.

She was doing it because she was afraid to ask Rosa.

It's spit, not surgery, she told herself, and slept.

## Chapter 4 — Results

The email came on a Tuesday, at 11:42 in the morning, between a call about a lost password and a call about a disappointing grandfather.

**Your OriginTree results are ready!**

Maya knew this email better than almost any text in her life. She had walked thousands of customers through it. *Click the green button, sir. Now you see a map.* She had never once stood on the other side of it.

She did not open it at work. This surprised her a little — she had imagined she would, between calls, casually, the way you check the weather. Instead she carried the email home on the two buses like something breakable, and ate dinner first, and washed the plate, and only then sat down with the laptop and clicked the green button.

A map of the world unfolded, dark blue, elegant. Then the circles began to bloom on it, one by one, the company's little theater of revelation. She watched her own existence become a pie chart.

**Eastern Mediterranean — approximately 61%.**
**Iberian Peninsula — approximately 22%.**
**Balkans — approximately 9%.**
**North Africa — approximately 5%.**
**Other — 3%.**

She sat looking at it for a long time.

She had explained to a thousand strangers what these numbers meant. Now she discovered she had no idea what they meant. They were correct, certainly — the science was real; she could recite the science — but they had the strange weightlessness of a fact about someone else. *Approximately 61%.* The word *approximately* had never bothered her before. Tonight it seemed to be the only honest word on the screen. The past, measured to the nearest whim.

There was more. The newer feature, the one the company was proudest of: **Ancestral Journeys**. The algorithm compared your DNA to its great library of the dead and the living, and drew you a path. *We can trace your ancestry,* said the page, *to a community of families that lived, two to three centuries ago, here* —

and the map zoomed.

It flew down out of the dark blue, past the boot of Italy, eastward, and landed on a piece of coast where two seas leaned toward each other and almost touched. A thin ribbon of water between them. A strait — with a name Maya had to read twice, a name that meant nothing to her at all, that rang no bell in any room of her memory. On its northern shore, the algorithm had placed a glowing dot.

*Your ancestors likely lived in or near this port city for many generations,* said the text, in the warm font the company used for emotions. *DNA from this community appears strongly in your results.*

Maya looked at the dot. The dot looked back, glowing, certain, smug.

A city. An actual city, with a name, on an actual strait. Generations of people in it whose faces had funneled down the centuries and arrived, by way of two buses, at this kitchen table. The screen knew this. Her own mother, Maya was fairly sure, did not know this. She herself, champion of the fourth floor, 4.9, first in the ranks for seven months, explainer of origins to the nation — had not known this at 11:41 this morning.

She did what her customers did. She googled the city.

Photographs: a harbor, hills, white buildings stacked above the water, gulls. Old photographs too, sepia ones — fishing boats, a market, women in long dresses on a steep street. And in nearly every photograph, on the high ground above the harbor, the same shape: heavy pale walls, a tower, the unmistakable stubborn geometry of a fortress.

The caption called it the citadel.

---

She went south on Thursday instead of waiting for Sunday, with the laptop in her bag and a speech in her head. The speech had several versions. In all of them she was calm and adult, and in all of them the conversation went well.

Rosa was in the garden, at war with the mint, which had crossed some border during the week. She looked up, and her face did the thing it always did when Maya appeared — opened, simply, like a door into a lit room.

"Thursday," Rosa said. "Who died?"

"Nobody died, Savta."

"Then sit, and we'll find a reason."

There was lemonade in a clay jug. The toad was in his shade. The afternoon was warm and ordinary, and Maya understood, with each passing minute, that every version of her speech had been written for some other, theoretical grandmother. Twice she began. The first time she got as far as "Savta, at work — " before Rosa sent her to bring the good glasses. The second time, Rosa was telling the story of the orange-tree murder at number twelve, and interrupting it was simply not physically possible.

Finally Maya just opened the laptop on the garden table, among the lemonade rings, and turned it around.

"What is this?" said Rosa.

"It's me. It's a test from my company. From spit. It shows where the family comes from."

Rosa looked at the map with the polite, distant interest she gave to all screens, as if they were aquariums.

"Sixty-one percent," she read. "Of what?"

"Of — the region. The eastern Mediterranean."

"For this they needed your spit? This I could tell them from your eyebrows." She pushed the laptop gently back, half a centimeter, a diplomatic expulsion. "Drink. The ice is melting."

"Savta." Maya turned the screen back. Her heart was working harder than the situation seemed to require. "It found a city. It says the family lived there. Look — here, by the strait."

And she watched.

She watched her grandmother's eyes go to the glowing dot, and stop, and stay. She watched the garden noise continue around a sudden zone of stillness, one meter wide, with a small old woman at the center of it. Rosa's face did not change, exactly. Something behind it changed. A door that had stood open for eighty years was very quietly drawn shut.

"Computers," Rosa said. "They print a map, people cry. It's a business, Mayaleh. You work there, you know best of all what it is."

"Is it true? Did the family live there?"

"The family lives here." Rosa's hand, when she lifted the jug, did not shake at all — and that, somehow, was the most frightening thing, the effort of all that steadiness. "From far, I told you. Far is far. Drink."

Maya drank. The conversation moved on, was moved on, firmly, the way traffic is moved past an accident. Rosa told two stories, criticized one government, and sent Maya to pick lemons for the road.

It was at the green gate, at the very end, in the gold light, that it happened. Rosa held Maya's face in both hands, as she always did. But she held it one moment longer than always, and her eyes went somewhere over Maya's shoulder, somewhere far past the street, and she said, in a voice Maya had never heard her use, a voice without any defenses in it at all:

"There was a citadel. Above the water. White, in the morning. From the boat it was the last thing you could see."

Then the door behind her face closed again, and she patted Maya's cheek, and said, "Eat something that isn't coffee," and lifted the green gate with her hip, and was gone behind it among her tomatoes.

Maya stood in the street with a bag of lemons.

The laptop had said *approximately*. The laptop had said *likely*. Rosa had said *from the boat it was the last thing you could see* — and there was nothing approximate in it, nothing likely. It was not data. It was a window, open for four seconds, eighty years deep.

All the way home, on both buses, Maya held the bag of lemons on her knees and thought one thought, around and around:

She never told me she came on a boat.

## Chapter 5 — What Rosa Won't Say

Maya's mother answered the phone the way she answered all phones, with suspicion first and identity second.

"Yes? Maya? What happened?"

"Nothing happened. Why does everyone in this family think the phone is a hospital?"

"Because in this family, historically, it was. So? Talk."

Maya talked. The test, the map, the dot on the strait, the city, the citadel. Her mother listened — Maya could hear her listening, could hear dishes stop moving at the other end — and when Maya reached the green gate, and the boat, and *the last thing you could see*, there was a silence with a long history inside it.

"She said that? About the boat?"

"Word for word."

"To you she said this." It was not jealousy in her mother's voice. It was something more complicated — the sound of a person hearing that a door, locked to them their whole life, had opened four seconds for somebody else.

"Ima. What do you know?"

"What do I know." Her mother laughed without any laughter in it. "I know what fits in a sentence, because that's how it was given out. Sentences. One a decade, if you behaved."

But she stayed on the phone, and slowly, the way water finds a crack, it came out — not the family's story, which she did not have, but the story of trying to get it, which she had in full.

She told Maya about being eleven, in fourth grade, when the teacher assigned a family tree. *Roots,* the homework was called. Bring photos. Interview your grandparents. Half her class came back with trees like forests, branches over branches, great-uncles hanging from every twig. She had come home to Rosa — *we lived with her then, your grandfather was already gone* — with her notebook and her sharpened pencil, official, proud, an interviewer.

"And?"

"And it was the only time in my life she shouted at me. The only time. Over homework." Her mother's voice had gone strange and young. "Then she saw my face and stopped, and she sat down, and she gave me — sentences. There was a city. There was the sea. They came when she was sixteen. Finished. I drew a tree with one branch and the teacher thought I hadn't done the work."

After that, her mother said, it became the war of her teenage years. She would push; Rosa would close. She would shout that she had a right to know where she came from; Rosa would say, in that immovable voice, *You come from this kitchen.* Their bickering over the past lasted years and settled nothing; it just slowly wore itself smooth, the way the sea wears glass, until they stopped, by unspoken treaty, and never started again.

"You know what she told me once? The closest she ever came?" Her mother breathed. "I screamed at her — I was maybe seventeen — I screamed, *Everyone has ancestors, everyone, even you.* And she looked at me and said: 'Ancestors are a luxury, Dina. We left in a hurry. We took what we could carry.'"

Maya sat with the phone against her ear and her eyes closed.

"There's a candle," her mother said, quieter now. "You've seen it, you just never noticed it. Every year, end of October, no date on any calendar of ours. She lights it in the kitchen and doesn't say kaddish, doesn't say anything, won't say who it's for. Once, only once, when I was small and didn't know fear yet, I asked. She said: 'For the brothers.' Two words. Fifty years I've been turning them over."

"Brothers. Hers?"

"I don't know, Maya. I don't know if they died or lived. I don't know if they got on a different boat or stayed on the dock. I asked Aunt Sima before she passed — she came from the same city, they knew each other from there — and Sima said only: 'It was a mess at the port. Lists, no lists. Families lost track of each other in an afternoon. Whoever tells you they remember it in order is lying.'" A pause. "And then Sima said something I never forgot. She said: 'Your mother doesn't refuse to remember, Dinaleh. She refuses to remember *out loud*. It's not the same disease.'"

Outside Maya's window, the city made its evening noise. On her laptop, asleep on the table, was a glowing dot that knew the latitude of all of this and none of it.

"Ima. Why didn't you ever just — keep digging? Records, archives. You could have."

The silence this time was her mother choosing honesty; Maya could hear the choice being made.

"Because she built us a whole world with her two hands," her mother said. "The house, the garden, the wheel, us. All of it on top of that silence, like a house on a sealed floor. And I decided — somewhere I decided — that the silence was load-bearing. You don't dig under your own house, Maya." A beat. "Also she's my mother and she shouted at me once in 1974 and I'm still not over it. Write that down too."

---

That Sunday, the soup needed nothing, as always. Rosa was tired, more than usual; after lunch she sat in the wicker chair under the fig tree and her eyes closed in the middle of a sentence about the municipality. Maya covered her knees with the thin blanket and sat on the step, watching her sleep.

Old age, Maya thought, had two hands. With one hand it took — words, names, the borders of the week. And with the other hand, sometimes, while no one watched, it gave things back.

Because Rosa was talking in her sleep. Not loudly. A murmur, in a language Maya didn't know — not the one Rosa cursed in, another one, older, with soft worn edges — and her face under the wrinkles was moving the way water moves under ice. She did not reminisce when awake; everyone knew that about her; she was famous for it. The neighbors' grandmothers ran reminiscence like a daily broadcast — *in our village, in those days* — and Rosa would snort and say the past was a country with bad service and she didn't vacation there. But asleep, under the fig tree, with the toad watching from his green dark, she was apparently there. Whatever there was. Her hand on the blanket opened and closed, opened and closed — holding something, or letting it go, sixty years a week.

Then she said it. Clearly, the way a word will suddenly surface whole out of murmur:

"Estreya."

Maya went still.

"Estreya," Rosa said again — and her voice cracked open on the middle of the name, and out of the crack came a tenderness so bare, so young, that Maya looked away from her sleeping face, the way you look away from someone undressed. "Estreya, dame la mano. Ya viene el vapor."

Then the murmur sank back below the surface, and the garden was just a garden, bees and afternoon, an old woman asleep in a chair.

Maya did not move for a long time. She had no idea what the words meant. She would not, in fact, look them up until that night, on the bus, with the bag of lemons on her knees — would not until then learn that they were in the old Spanish her ancestors had carried out of Iberia and kept alive for five hundred years in a port city on a strait, and that they meant:

*Estreya, give me your hand. The boat is coming.*

She called her mother from the bus.

"Ima. Who is Estreya?"

And her mother, who knew the name of no one in that vanished city, who had a family tree with one branch, said slowly:

"I have no idea. But you just found the candle."

## Chapter 6 — Decay

In June, Rosa put a pot of soup on the stove and went out to the garden, and forgot the soup, and forgot the stove, and the neighbor smelled smoke at four in the afternoon.

Nothing burned, in the end, except the pot and the soup and a certain story the family had been telling itself. The kitchen wall wore a tongue of black above the stove. Rosa was furious — at the pot, at the neighbor, at the entire concept of the fire department — and her fury was so familiar, so completely her, that for an hour everyone almost managed to laugh about it.

Then Maya's mother found, in the drawer under the telephone, four electricity bills, unopened, in their envelopes, arranged in a neat and meaningless stack. And on the back of one envelope, in Rosa's handwriting, a list:

*Dina — daughter
Maya — Dina's
Thursday — garbage
The blue pills — morning*

People keep track of what they are losing, in the order they are losing it.

---

What came next, Maya would remember as a single long corridor.

It was not a single corridor. It was many — the health fund, the hospital, the memory clinic, the National Insurance office — but they pooled in her memory into one fluorescent hallway with a queue number machine at the end of it. Take a ticket. Wait. The board blinks. *Now serving 304.* You are 351.

She discovered that the world she worked in had a twin, and the twin was darker. Here too there were scripts. Here too there were hold lines — she spent, by her own count, eleven hours that summer listening to the health fund's hold music, a loop of synthetic strings that she began to hear in her sleep. *Your call is important to us. You are number eight in the queue.* Once, waiting, she caught herself evaluating the agent who finally answered — the pacing, the empty warmth, the *I understand* placed like furniture — and realized she was listening to herself, and had to sit down.

There were questionnaires. God, there were questionnaires. Dense pages of small print that asked in forty ways whether her grandmother was still a person. *Can the patient dress herself? Prepare food? Manage money? Is the patient a hazard to herself or others?*

That word. *Hazard.* Maya filled in the forms at her kitchen table at night and stopped at that word every time. A hazard was a wet floor. A hazard was a loose wire. Her grandmother had crossed a sea at sixteen with one suitcase and built a world out of clay and tomatoes, and now she was being filed under the same word as a pothole.

At the memory clinic — the venue was a health-fund branch that had clearly once been a bank, the doctors' rooms still wearing the heavy doors of vaults — a kind, tired neurologist gave Rosa the test. Maya sat in the corner, instructed not to help.

"What year is it, Rosa?"

"A bad one."

"And the season?"

"Ask my tomatoes, they keep track."

"I'm going to say three words, and ask you to repeat them later. Apple. Table. Coin."

"Apple, table, coin." A wave of the hand. "A poor man's shopping list."

She knew the year, in the end, after a pause that everyone in the room pretended not to measure. She did not know the date. She drew the clock with all twelve numbers crowded into the right half of the circle, like passengers on a listing boat. And when the neurologist, ten minutes later, asked for the three words again, Rosa looked at her with her chin high and her eyes narrow, the look of a citadel above a harbor, and said:

"They've gone. Write that I let them go."

In the corridor afterward, the neurologist spoke to Maya and her mother in the low, padded voice of a person delivering weather from a country you are moving to. The word *Alzheimer's* was used, with qualifiers — *early*, *mild*, *we will watch it*. A rate was discussed, with the honesty of a tired good doctor: *every patient declines at her own rate; some go quickly; some hold a plateau for years, whole good years; we treat, we slow, we don't promise.* There was a pamphlet, and a follow-up date, and the strange double gift of a name for the thing and no date attached to it.

Her mother drove home with both hands high on the wheel, silent. At a red light she said, "She'll cling to that house with her fingernails. You know that. She'd rather burn with it."

"So we make sure it doesn't burn," Maya said. "We don't make her leave the garden, Ima. The garden is half her memory. It's the half that still works."

---

But the garden knew.

That was the thing Maya could not have explained to the neurologist, to the forms, to anyone who had not stood in it. The garden declined at the rate of its gardener. Rosa forgot the watering one day in two, then two days in three; the municipal summer leaned in; and the green world behind the gate began, quietly, to step back. The mint surrendered first, going gray at the edges like paper held near a flame. The tomato plants stopped setting fruit, and the fruit they had they let drop off, unripe, into the dust — small hard green planets, a crop abandoning its planet's star.

Maya came now three times a week, then four, with her sleeves rolled, dragging the hose down the rows in the evening heat, watching the water twist away into the cracked soil as into an open drain. She was not a gardener; she had never been; she had been a visitor in this Eden for twenty-six years, eating its fruit, knowing nothing. Now she learned by trial and error, in the worst possible school. Some things came back. Some things did not. Under the fig tree, where the shade was still dense and the fallen figs went soft and dark, the smell of ripeness slid one degree past itself into the smell of decay — that sweet brown borderline smell — and the wasps held their slow parliament above it, and the toad sat in his cool kingdom, golden-eyed, watching the empire fail around him.

Rosa came out less. She watched from the wicker chair, wrapped in a cardigan in August, and gave orders that were sometimes commands and sometimes memories of commands. *Water deep, not often. Pinch the suckers. Not that, that's not a weed, that's oregano* — this last when Maya was holding a weed and nowhere near the oregano, and they both knew it, and neither said it.

One evening at the end of August, when the watering was done and the light lay along the ground in copper bars, Rosa was quiet for so long that Maya thought she had drifted off. Then she saw that her grandmother was looking at her own hands, turned palm-up in her lap, as at two old letters in a language she was beginning to lose.

"Mayaleh."

"Savta."

"The hands forget last," Rosa said. She said it as a fact, the way she would say the soup needs five minutes. "Everything else is a colander. The hands forget last."

She closed her fingers, opened them.

"Sunday," she said. "Come early. Wear something with bad sleeves."

"Bad sleeves? Why?"

But Rosa was looking past her, at the dark back room of the house, where for fifteen years a covered wheel had stood under a sheet, holding its shape in the dark like everything else she refused to say out loud.

"Because Sunday," said Rosa, "I'm teaching you the clay."

## Chapter 7 — The Clay

The sheet came off the wheel the way sheets come off furniture in old houses: with a breath of dust and the sudden presence of a sleeping thing. The wheel was smaller than Maya remembered. Everything from childhood was. It sat there, iron and stone, patient, like the toad.

Rosa had risen early and was more here than she had been in weeks — the morning hours were her good hours now; the family had learned to schedule love around them like flights. She wore her old apron, stiff with fifteen-year-old clay. She had made Maya roll her sleeves past the elbow, examined the result, and rolled them higher.

The clay itself lived in a plastic-wrapped block in the corner, and the first surprise was that it was alive. Not a way of speaking — workably, stubbornly alive: it had to be cut, with a wire held in two hands, and the portion Rosa cut came away smooth and dense as cheese, cool as a cellar. She slapped it down on the table and showed Maya how to wedge it, pressing and folding, pressing and folding, driving the air out.

"Air is the enemy," Rosa said. "A bubble hides inside, quiet, polite, you see nothing. Then the fire finds it." She brought her palm down. "Boom. The whole pot, gone, and its neighbors in the kiln too. What is hidden does not stay small, Mayaleh. In the fire everything hidden gets a vote."

She said it about the clay. She said everything about the clay, all morning. That was the system, and Maya understood it within the hour: the clay was a country where they could say anything, and both of them had visas.

The wheel turned. Rosa's foot remembered the rhythm before her face did — Maya saw it happen, saw the body arrive somewhere ahead of the mind, the way her own hands typed greetings she no longer heard. *The hands forget last.* Rosa took the wedged clay, threw it at the center of the wheel, cupped it, and Maya watched fifteen years of retirement fall away in approximately four seconds.

"Now you."

It was a catastrophe, and the catastrophe was the lesson.

Centering, it turned out, was everything, and centering was nearly impossible. The clay spun under Maya's hands and threw her off like a small planet rejecting a settler. If it sat even slightly off the center of the wheel, every turn made it worse: the smallest protrusion, a bump you could barely feel, grew with each revolution, wobbled, widened, until the whole thing lurched drunkenly and collapsed over her fingers.

"You see?" Rosa was delighted. Failure, on the wheel, delighted her; it was the curriculum. "A small thing off-center — you think, it's nothing, I'll fix it later. But the wheel turns, and turns, and every turn the nothing gets bigger. Sixty turns, it's a wave. A hundred, it's a flood. On the wheel there is no later, Mayaleh. There is only now, now, now." She put her hands over Maya's hands — strong, certain, the skin loose over the certainty — and steadied them against the spinning clay. "Don't fight it. Hold still and let it find you. You are the center. Be boring. The clay loves boring."

By noon, Maya had destroyed eleven lumps of clay and made one object. It could be called a bowl by a generous court. Its walls were thick on one side, thin on the other; its rim traveled; it had a lean, like a listing boat.

She learned the vocabulary of repair: a touch of water, a tweak of pressure with one knuckle inside the curve, the rib tool drawn up the wall to coax it straight — *gently, you're not the police.* She learned that water was a loan, not a gift; too much and the walls drank themselves soft and fell. She learned that pottery was nothing but trial and error so old it had become wisdom: every rule Rosa recited — and she recited them like scripture — was the scar of ten thousand broken pots, hers and her teachers' and the dead's. "Glaze, we won't get to today," Rosa said, eyeing the leaning bowl. "But know: a good glaze hides nothing. It enhances what's there. Lies are for paint."

And somewhere in the middle of all of it — Maya could never afterward say exactly when, because there was no announcement, no breath taken, no *I will now tell you* — somewhere between one ruined cylinder and the next, with the wheel humming and both their hands in the wet, Rosa began to talk.

"My father had a workshop two streets above the harbor," she said, hands cupped around spinning clay. "Pots, tiles, jars for oil. Oil and fish, that was the staple there; everything in that city was for oil and fish. The clay came on donkeys from the hills. After rain, the whole street smelled like this. Exactly like this. You opened the window and the city smelled like a wheel."

Maya did not move. She had the feeling you have near a wild animal that has come close on its own: that the world depended on her stillness.

"I was at the wheel from six years old. My brothers had no patience for it — Moshiko wanted the boats, Albert wanted the girls — but me, the wheel. And Estreya." The name came out level, workmanlike, a name among names; only Rosa's foot on the pedal changed rhythm for half a turn. "Estreya was small. Seven years under me. She would sit there — there, like you, exactly there — and her job was the water. She held the sponge. The minister of water, my father called her. Estreya means star, in our Spanish. She was born at night, so, a star."

The wheel hummed. The walls of the pot rose under Rosa's fingers, even and sure.

"In October it ended. I won't tell you the politics; the politics you can read; the politics has books." A wave of one muddy hand at all the books. "What has no books is the port. One afternoon. Everyone at once, and the lists not matching the boats, and the boats not matching the names, and a man at a table with a stamp deciding the future of the world one family at a time. We had papers for one vapor — one boat — all six of us. Then we had papers for two boats. Then for a boat and a maybe. My mother is shouting at the man with the stamp. My father is holding the money inside his coat. And I am sixteen, and I am holding Estreya's hand, because that is my whole job in the world, the minister of water, my job."

The pot rose. It was the best pot of the day, and Rosa was not looking at it at all.

"She saw something. A dog, a soldier's horse, I never knew — sixty years, you understand, I have had time to not know. She pulled. Children pull. And the suitcase was falling off the cart on my other side, the suitcase with the photographs and the candlesticks, the whole house in one suitcase — and I let go of her hand to catch it."

The wheel turned. Now, now, now.

"One minute," Rosa said. "Less. I caught the suitcase. The crowd moved like water. And the crowd put my mother and my father and me against the rope of our boat, and it put Moshiko and Albert and Estreya twenty meters away, behind the barrier, with my aunt. Twenty meters. And the man with the stamp had finished being kind that day."

Her foot eased. The wheel slowed, the hum descending.

"The second boat had a name. I knew it sixty years and now, some mornings, I don't — it comes back at night, names come back at night. It was to leave in three days. From the deck of ours I saw them on the dock — small, like this —" she held up a muddy thumb and finger, a centimeter of air between them, "— and above them the citadel, white, in the morning sun. The last thing you could see. I told you that part already." A pause. "I waved. Estreya didn't wave. She was looking for my hand in the crowd, you understand. At the wrong height. She was looking for a hand."

The wheel stopped.

"Three days later, the politics closed the port," Rosa said, in the voice of someone reading a label. "The second boat never left. We wrote letters to the city for two years. The city stopped answering. After the war we wrote to committees, to lists, to the Red Cross — your great-grandmother wrote until her hands stopped, and then I wrote. There was no trace. You looked for one name in that fire, you got back silence with a file number. In '52 a man from our street found us here — he had been on the dock those three days. He said the brothers had tried to put Estreya on a fishing boat across the strait, to cousins on the other side. He saw them pay. He didn't see the boat go. That is the whole archive, Mayaleh. A man saw them pay."

She looked at her hands, mud to the wrist.

"At the clinic, the doctor with the three words. Apple, table, coin." Something moved through Rosa's face that was almost, terribly, a smile. "Sixty years I keep three names instead, and a boat, and a hand. Now the colander is losing the year and the date and the prime minister, who is no loss. And I think — every morning I check, like you check a pocket — what goes first. Because if her name goes while I am alive to know it went —"

She did not finish. She did not need to finish. She took the best pot of the day off the wheel with the wire, in one clean pull, and set it on the board next to Maya's leaning bowl, and looked at the two of them, the masterpiece and the catastrophe, side by side.

"Yours we keep," she said.

"Savta, it's crooked."

"It's crooked," Rosa agreed. "So is everything that survives."

They cleaned up slowly, in the particular silence of after, scraping the wheel, washing to the elbow, the water running gray-brown into the drain and the smell of wet clay rising off everything like the smell of a street in a drowned city. On the bus home, Maya held her bowl in both hands, wrapped in newspaper, still damp, the only object she owned that contained the entire afternoon — the port, the citadel, the centimeter of air between a thumb and a finger. Her laptop at home held the dot, the map, the percentages. This was heavier. This was the past the way Rosa had always kept it: not spoken, not written.

Tangible.

That night, for the first time in her life, Maya lit a candle in October, in her own kitchen, and sat with it, and did not say anything, and began — at last, at the wrong height, in the wrong century — to look for a hand in the crowd.

## Chapter 8 — Down the Line

There was one bad night that winter, and it has to be told, because of what happened in it.

In January Rosa caught a fever — an ordinary winter fever, the doctor said, nothing, fluids and rest — but fever, in a mind already loosened at the edges, opens doors. Maya was staying over; they took turns now, she and her mother, Sundays and Tuesdays and the nights after hard days. At three in the morning she woke to a sound and found Rosa sitting up in bed, hot, lost somewhere without coordinates, her arm out, her hand opening and closing on the dark.

"Estreya," Rosa said. Not frightened. Urgent, organized, sixteen years old: "Estreya, dame la mano. Ya viene el vapor."

And Maya — who had practiced the sentence on two buses for months without ever knowing why, the way you keep a key to a house you've never seen — crossed the room and put her hand into the opening and closing hand, at exactly the right height, and answered in her bad new old Spanish:

"Aquí está. Aquí está la mano." *Here it is. Here is the hand.*

Rosa's fingers closed. Her whole body let go of something — Maya felt it leave, an alertness sixty years on duty going off shift — and she lay back, and slept until morning, holding on.

By the end of the week the fever was gone and Rosa was back at the stove, scandalized by what Maya had done to a soup in her absence. But Maya kept the night. She understood, sitting in that dark with her arm going numb and refusing to move it, that she had been allowed to repair a single second of the port — one second out of all the seconds, the one with the suitcase and the hand. The wheel turns and there is no later. But it turns, Rosa had never said, in a circle.

---

The illness, having announced itself, then did the thing the tired good doctor had said it sometimes does: it slowed down and held.

Not a recovery — there are no recoveries; the year still went missing some mornings, and the date had emigrated for good, and the names of distant cousins now arrived a beat late, like an overseas call. But a plateau. Whole good months. The household reorganized itself around the good hours the way the garden organized itself around the sun: mornings were for the wheel and the important talk; afternoons for the wicker chair; pills lived in a plastic box with the days printed on it, and the days were crossed off by three different hands.

The third hand was Dina's.

Maya's mother had begun, in the spring, to come on Thursdays — at first with the face of a person performing a duty, armored to the chin. But the garden, it turned out, worked on her the way the clay had worked on Rosa: it was a country where things could be said sideways. Rosa, who had never managed to teach her daughter anything without the lesson catching fire, taught her, at seventy-nine, to pinch tomato suckers — guiding her hand, grunting approval — and Dina, fifty-eight years old, glowed like a girl with a good report card and pretended not to.

"All those years," Dina said to Maya once, watching Rosa inspect their work down the row. "I thought she was keeping the past from us. She wasn't, you know. She was standing between us and it. There's a difference. It took me until this garden to find it."

---

Maya went back to her full shifts in March, and the fourth floor was exactly where she had left it: the queue breathing on the screen, the cartoon sun over the coffee machine, the board with the ranks. Her name had slipped to fourth while she worked half-days — **M. ALON**, in modest white letters — and she looked at it and waited to feel something and felt, with relief, nothing at all.

It was there, on a Tuesday, between a call about a lost password and a call about a disappointing grandfather, that the email came.

**You have a new DNA relative!**

The algorithm, grinding patiently through its growing library of the world's spit, had connected a thread. *Estimated 3rd–4th cousin,* it said. A username, no photo, an account registered across the strait — the far shore, the one a fishing boat had once been paid to reach, where the cousins had lived. *Shared DNA: 1.1%.* Approximately, of course. Likely. Possibly.

It was nothing. It was a rounding error of history; she of all people knew how little it might mean; she had explained that very smallness to a thousand strangers, gently, sixty times a day. The records that could have confirmed anything were gone — a century's worth of destruction had eaten the port's lists, the synagogue books, the city itself as it had been — and over all that ash, one algorithm had quietly laid one thread.

She sat looking at it for a long time. Then she wrote, in simple English, a message of four sentences. She gave her grandmother's first name and the name of the city. She did not tell the story; the story was not hers to spend. The last sentence took her until the end of her shift, and in the end it was this: *My grandmother had a little sister. We never stopped looking — we just lost track of how.*

She pressed send. And then came the part nobody puts on the posters: the waiting.

It was Mr. Mandelbaum, of all the people in the world, who got her through it. He called in April with a small official question — 2% Caucasus, was it the mountains — and she answered it for the fifth time, and then, because the queue was short and because some walls had come down in her that year and stayed down, she told him: that she had sent a message across the sea, and that it had been three weeks, and that she checked every morning like a pocket.

"Three weeks," said Mr. Mandelbaum, with the deep professional calm of a man who had waited for letters in four decades when letters were paper. "Listen to me, champion. A message like that doesn't get read. It gets *carried around*. Somebody over there is walking with it now, to a sister, to an aunt, to the one in the family who keeps the names — every family has one, the minister of memory. It will answer when it has finished traveling. You taught me this yourself: people are slow when something is heavy." A pause, and she could hear him smiling. "My daughter, by the way. The tube. She spat — two weeks after we spoke that time. Four percent something nobody expected; she called me laughing from Toronto at her own midnight. That was your doing, down the line."

"I just answered a call, Mr. Mandelbaum."

"Yes," he said. "That's the whole profession, if anyone asks."

---

The reply came in May, at night, and Maya read it standing up in her kitchen and then sat down on the floor and read it again.

It was from a woman on the far shore, the granddaughter of the username, writing in careful, borrowed English. *Forgive my language. My grandmother's name was Estreya. She came to my family's village in the war, a child alone off a fishing boat, with a paper pinned inside her coat. The paper had her name and the name of her father's workshop. Our great-grandfather knew the workshop. He kept her.*

She lived. That was the whole of it and the size of it. She grew up on the other side of the strait, in a hill town above the water, in sight — Maya checked the map and her breath went strange — in literal sight, on clear days, of the city she had been put on a boat to escape. She married. There were children; there were the children's children; there was, the woman wrote, a garden — *figs, you should see the figs, they are a scandal.* She had died fifteen years ago, in her sleep, an old woman with her family around her like a crop.

Of the brothers, the letter knew nothing. They had put her on the boat and stepped back into the crowd, and the crowd had kept them. The letter did not pretend otherwise, and Maya was grateful to it for that.

But there were two more things, and they were the two that undid her.

The first: *She told us always, she had a big sister over the sea who could make the wheel sing. When we did not believe a person can make a wheel sing, she became angry.*

The second: *Every year in October my grandmother lit a candle and would not say for whom. We thought it was for her parents. Now I think you and I have been lighting the same candle from two sides of the same water, and nobody knew.*

There was a photograph attached. An old woman in a garden, laughing at something outside the frame, one hand raised against the sun, standing under a fig tree.

---

Maya told Rosa on a Sunday morning, in the good hours, at the kitchen table, with the laptop closed; she had decided the screen would not be in the room. She told it the way Rosa had taught her things are told: plainly, in order, with her hands flat on the wood. The boat that crossed. The paper pinned in the coat. The hill town in sight of the citadel. The figs. The long life. The sleep at the end of it, the family around her like a crop. The sister over the sea who could make the wheel sing.

Rosa sat completely still through all of it, her eyes on Maya's hands, and when it was finished she was silent for so long that the kitchen clock became loud.

"Sixty years I asked the dark," she said at last, in a voice that came from very far down. "It knew the whole time. It could have said." Then, in nearly the same breath, because she was Rosa: "Did she have a garden?"

"Figs. The granddaughter says they're a scandal."

"Of course figs." Rosa closed her eyes. Under the wrinkles, something moved like water under ice, and settled. "She held the sponge," she said, to no one, to the table, to a workshop two streets above a harbor. "The minister of water. Of course a garden."

She did not cry, or if she did, it was later and in private, in the manner of citadels. What she did instead was practical, and total, and it happened in October.

That year, when the unmarked date came around, Rosa did not light the candle alone in a silent kitchen. She had the family in — Dina, Maya, the neighbor from number eight who had brought soup for ten years without commentary — and she lit two candles, and for the first time in anyone's life she said names over them, out loud, in a steady voice: her brothers' names, into the wax of the first, still searching; and over the second, the name that had been kept sixty years in the dark like a seed in a jar:

"Estreya. Who lived."

The flame leaned and stood. And somewhere across a strait, on a hill in sight of a white citadel, the same small light was being set in a window by people the family had never met and now, slowly, in two careful languages, two photographs a month, was beginning to.

---

The story does not end with a journey, though there may be one down the line — the granddaughter has written *the door of our house is open; the sea is small*, and Maya has begun, on Thursday evenings, to learn a portion of the old Spanish from recordings, so that if she ever stands in that garden she can say more than here is the hand. The story does not end with a cure, because there is none; the colander is a colander; there will be harder years, and everyone in the small house behind the green gate knows their shape already and has decided to be there for them anyway, which is most of what deciding is.

The story ends where it started, on a Sunday, in the garden.

The crop came back crooked that summer — Maya's rows would never be Rosa's rows — but it came back: tomatoes splitting their skins again in the heat, mint reconquering its old empire, the fig tree budding with the total, amnesiac confidence of fig trees. Rosa supervised from the wicker chair, issuing commands that were sometimes commands and sometimes memories of commands, and was obeyed either way. The toad sat in his dense green shade, golden-eyed, the most reliable resident in the neighborhood, keeping the only records he had ever kept, which were enough: shade, water, flies, a view.

On the kitchen windowsill stood two bowls. Rosa's last masterpiece was not one of them. One was the small pinched bowl her hands had made one quiet afternoon without any help from the rest of her. The other was Maya's famous catastrophe — thick on one side, thin on the other, its rim a traveler, its lean like a listing boat — holding, that summer, a handful of seeds for next year, because that is what vessels are for, and it held them.

It was crooked. It held them anyway.

Somewhere in the city to the north, a queue was blinking, and strangers were waiting in it to be told who they were, and the telephones were ringing down all the lines. Here there was water moving into soil, and two women arguing pleasantly about oregano, and the smell of wet earth rising from the rows like the smell of a street above a harbor — and the garden, which had been spoken to in soup and silence for fifty years and had finally been answered, leaned toward summer with its whole body, and did what it had always done over every silence and every sea, which was thrive.

**The end.**
